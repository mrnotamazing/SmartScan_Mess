# Making students care about the wait: ideas for the door screen

The screen only works if students act on it. Most will walk past it on autopilot, toward their friends and their usual mess.
These ideas come from behavioural economics and consumer psychology. **Built** means it's in the demo screen now
(`/screen?at=A`). **Next** means it's worth building and A/B testing in the pilot.

The rule above all of them: **never lie on the screen.** One wrong "go to B, it's empty" costs weeks of trust.
Every nudge below uses true numbers from scans and the mess manager's food-status taps.

## 1. Colour: use it for meaning, not decoration

| Idea | Why it works | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Green / amber / red status with an icon and a word (● Short wait, ▲ Busy, ■ Very busy) | Traffic-light colours are understood instantly, before anyone reads. Icon + word keeps it readable for colour-blind students (about 1 in 12 men) | Built |
| The whole hero panel is tinted: green for "come on in", amber for "busy", blue for "go to the other mess" | Colour is read from across a corridor, text isn't. Blue is an action colour that doesn't feel like a warning | Built |
| **No red hero, ever.** Red appears only on a hall's status, not as the main message | A big red screen outside the door they're already at makes students feel stuck and annoyed, not helped. Red is useful only when there's something to do about it | Built |
| A recommended card gets a blue outline and a "Recommended" tag | A visible default makes the choice for the undecided (default effect) | Built |
| Fullness meters like a car-park sign ("97% full · space for ~9") | Students already trust "spaces left" signs at malls. The number comes straight from scans, so it's the most accurate thing on screen | Built |

## 2. Framing the numbers

| Idea | Why it works | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Show **time to food**, not "wait time" ("~3 min to food") | People care about when they eat, not about an abstract wait | Built |
| Side-by-side comparison: "Food in hand in ~3 min there, vs ~9 min here" | A wait means nothing without something to compare it to. The contrast does the persuading | Built |
| Turn minutes into something concrete: "☕ Enough time for a chai" | Concrete gains beat abstract ones; "6 minutes" is forgettable, a chai break isn't | Built |
| The walk is shown honestly ("30 s walk") | Students overestimate effort; showing it's only 30 seconds removes the main objection | Built |
| Soft vs strong message: "In a hurry? Mess B is ~4 min faster" vs "Mess B is faster right now →" | Matches the probabilistic routing: a soft nudge moves only those in a rush, so the other mess doesn't flood | Built |
| Never suggest switching for less than 2 minutes of saving | A walk for nothing is the fastest way to lose trust | Built |

## 3. Food cues (probably the strongest motivator)

| Idea | Why it works | Status |
|---|---|---|
| "✨ Fresh batch just out" for 10 min after the manager taps "Refilled" | Fresh food pulls harder than a short line, and it turns a refill into a reason to go there | Built |
| "🍲 A tray is running low" | Honest warning so students aren't surprised inside | Built |
| Show the menu item that's fresh ("Fresh rotis just out") | More specific means more appetising | Next (needs the tray name on the screen) |

## 4. Social and habit

| Idea | Why it works | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Social proof: "Most students are heading to Mess B right now" | People follow what others do (descriptive norms) | Built on the hallway screen. At door screens it needs a measured switch count; don't fake it |
| "Going with friends? Mess B has space for groups" | Friends are the #1 reason students won't switch. Address it directly | Next (needs a table-occupancy estimate) |
| Weekly "minutes saved" stat on the screen ("This week the screens saved students 41 hours") | Makes the system feel like a shared win; supports adoption | Next (Phase 5 metrics) |
| Prosocial line, used sparingly: "Switching helps the whole line move faster" | Some students respond to helping others, but it's weaker than self-interest. Keep it secondary | Next, A/B test it |

## 5. Rewards (needs per-student data, so think about privacy first)

| Idea | Why it works | Status |
|---|---|---|
| "Balance points": switching when asked earns points toward a free tea or dessert | Small, certain rewards build a habit; the vendor gains because balanced halls waste less food | Phase 6. Needs consent and a per-student key |
| Priority lane at the less-busy mess for switchers | Turns the walk into a perk | Discuss with the vendor |

## 6. Timing and trust

| Idea | Why it works | Status |
|---|---|---|
| "Rush ends ~13:20. Come back after for no wait" | Shifts demand over time, not just between halls | Phase 6 (peak prediction) |
| A visible "updated 13:07" and "estimates from entry scans" | Honesty about precision makes people forgive small errors | Built |
| Track and show accuracy ("Our times were right 9 times out of 10 today") | Earned credibility | Phase 5 |

## What to A/B test in the pilot

Run each for a few days and compare the **switch rate** (more scans at the less-busy mess while the screen asks for it) and the **wait spread**:

1. Wait time only vs time-to-food comparison.
2. With vs without the chai line.
3. With vs without "Fresh batch just out".
4. Soft vs strong wording at the same saving.
5. Screen on vs screen off (the control). It shows whether the whole thing works.
